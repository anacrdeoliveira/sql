```
Back in the early 2000s, as a young student of physics, I remember hearing that "data does not lie". Back then, we were concerned about misleading sensations that led one to the wrong conclusion. For instance, to think that a blanket "produces heat" instead of conserving the heat one produces with their own body under said blanket. Nonetheless, even away from the context, this stuck with me for a great part of my life: feelings are misleading. Data = facts. Facts = truth.

Fast forward almost 20 years and here I was, a PhD student in a field of social sciences, learning about textual analysis within political science. How does the machine learn the "facts" that allow it to read a blog in infer whether that blog was written by a man or a woman? How often does the code get it right? My friend and I, two women taking the same summer class, both originally physicists, kept private texting ourselves about the so called truths the machine was learning and imposing on us: we were expected to like baking, babies, fashion. Not cars, not math. Not science. 

In the field of industrial relations, we often talk about bias in recruitment and selection. And many suggest that the solution to the problem of discrimination is asking AI to recruit, find the best candidate, filter resumes. Allegedly, AI is unbiased. In truth, I wonder how the machine could learn about the characteristics of a good engineer. I am very much aware that it is significantly more likely to be called back to an interview in the GTA if, everything else being equal interms of education and work experience, your name is John, or Greg, instead of Ali, or Hassan (Oreopoulos, 2011). A name, an aplicant_id type of data. 

Personally, I chose to keep my own name when I got married. I did not adopt my husband's name. I know that using the word "husband" instead of partner, has a political meaning. Culturally, in both my culture and his, women often change names when they are married. In Canada, the mother's maiden name is a common security question to validate one's id at a bank. But if I ever wished to change my name, I anticipated having to handle an unbearable amount of data complications concerning my ID.

In fact, when I needed to renew my OHIP card - such a privilege to have one! - I was blocked to do so because the system needed me to present my PR card. Well, I told the agent at service Ontario, I had to cut that one in half when I became a Canadian citizen! Sadly, he replied, the system did not allow him to renew my OHIP card unless I brought in a proof of citizenship. Mind you, I pay taxes, I have a passport, but the data service ontario has access to does not connect with the data that the CRA or even CIC have on me. At the end, the Service Ontario agent chose to help me out and gave me a new OHIP card under the agreement that I would bring him my proof of citizenship as soon as I could.

A few months ago, I had a similar problem with my bank account. I had to go in person to a branch to update my PR card expriry date (well, that old card I cut in half would have expired by then). The banking agent told me how she tricked the system: as it was not possible to change my status to "Canadian", Scotiabank listed my PR card expire date as 2099 (or somehting similar). It is fun to see how people may adapt and trick databases, which is also evidence that databases can be poorly designed.

The personal stories I shared above may be fun, because they are about simple inconveniences. But my friend had some trouble to get his Italian citizenship recognized because in Brazil, where we are from, once someone is married, their birth certificate data is deemed invalid. From the moment one is married, their fundamental document becomes their marriage certificate. However, because my friend is a man who married another man, his marriage certificate was not recognized by Italy, and as a consequence, he no longer had a valid essential document. He ceased to exist, apparently. Either that, or I have been wrong for all these years: data are not facts... 

Data, in fact, is produced by someone, who like everyone in the world, is embeded into a world of systemic discrimination. I believe we need to be constantly aware of that and create mechanisms to make data reflect and respect the truth of who we are. This will probably involve effort, practice, several errors, and a lot of rework. Hopefully, the burden will be placed on the hands of people like ourselves, who have opportunities to learn, and not on people like Riz or his father.
```
